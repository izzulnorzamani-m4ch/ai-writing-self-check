Fieldwork notes on recycling at my college

I spent three weeks in September counting what went into the bins behind Block C, because my supervisor said my proposal was too abstract. The first morning I got it wrong. I weighed the bags with the wet food still inside, so my plastic figure for that day (6.2 kg) is useless and I threw it out. After that Aiman helped me sort everything on a tarp before weighing.

Most of what we found was not what the posters warn about. Roughly 40% by weight was bubble tea cups with the liquid still in them, which the contractor told me they will not accept. I asked the cafe owner, Kak Lina, why she doesn't use a separate bin, and she laughed and said nobody had ever asked her to. That conversation changed my research question.

In today's rapidly evolving world, sustainability plays a pivotal role in shaping the landscape of higher education. As institutions navigate the complexities of waste management, stakeholders are increasingly recognizing the transformative potential of innovative recycling programmes. It is important to note that this shift is not merely an operational change but a fundamental reimagining of campus life.

Moreover, effective recycling initiatives foster collaboration, responsibility, and awareness among students from diverse backgrounds. By leveraging educational campaigns, universities can create engaging environments that cater to a wide range of learners, thereby enhancing environmental outcomes. Furthermore, the integration of data-driven systems offers valuable insights into waste streams, enabling administrators to provide timely and targeted interventions. This holistic approach underscores the importance of aligning policy with sound environmental principles.

Additionally, a comprehensive strategy serves as a cornerstone of long-term success, ensuring that progress is sustained across the institution. Such efforts not only strengthen institutional practice but also empower communities to adapt to changing needs. Ultimately, by fostering a culture of continuous improvement, universities can pave the way for a more sustainable and inclusive future.

Back at Block C, the numbers in week three surprised me. Once Kak Lina put out a bucket for leftover drinks, the contaminated share dropped from about 40% to 22%, though I only have five days of data and two of those were during a public holiday. I would not trust that figure yet. The contractor still rejected one load on 24 September because someone had dumped paint tins in the bin, and I never found out who did it. Next semester I want to repeat the count with a proper control block.

How this score works

The score is the share of your checked words that were flagged. Like Turnitin, it counts full paragraphs and leaves out references, headings, lists, short table text and captions. Each document needs 300 to 30,000 checked words. An automatic check looks for common AI wording, then Claude reads the text for sudden changes in style. Neither has a measured accuracy rate, and neither compares your text with other sources, so this is not a plagiarism check.
